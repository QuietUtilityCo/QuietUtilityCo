How to Run Better 1:1 Meetings as a Manager

September 15, 2026

1:1 meetings are the most important conversations you have as a manager, but many are derailed by unclear agendas, scope creep, and forgotten action items.

If you're managing a remote team, the stakes are higher. You don't have hallway time to build trust or catch early problems. Your 1:1s are often your only dedicated time with each person.

The problem is simple: without structure, 1:1s drift. You start with career development, get pulled into a technical problem mid-way, and end with vague next steps nobody remembers. By the next week, the same issues come up again.

The Core Structure That Works

Strong 1:1 meetings follow a predictable pattern. Each meeting should cover three areas:

  1. Previous Action Items (5 min) — What did you both commit to last time? What's done, what's stuck?
  2. Progress & Current Work (10 min) — How's the week going? Any blockers?
  3. Growth & Career (5 min) — What's developing? What do they want to learn?

This structure forces both of you to come prepared. It signals that you care about their development, not just output. And it creates a paper trail—literally, in writing—of what matters to them and how you're supporting it.

Most managers skip the third part. They run meetings that feel like status updates. Remote managers especially do this because they're already doing async standup updates. The 1:1 becomes "any blockers?" and hangs up. Then people feel unseen and start job hunting.

Why This Matters More for Remote Teams

When your team is distributed, casual feedback is gone. You don't notice that someone's quiet in meetings, or that they're stressed about a skills gap, or that they're being side-lined by an opinionated peer. You only see what they choose to show you.

1:1s fill that gap. But only if they're consistent and they go deep. A 30-minute call where you actually ask "What do you want to be better at in the next 6 months?" beats 10 Slack messages.

Remote-first companies with low turnover—Gitlab, Automattic, Basecamp—are obsessive about 1:1 structure. They publish templates. They timebox ruthlessly. They write summaries after each call. Because they know that without structure, a distributed team frays.

The Checklist That Keeps You Honest

Here's what your 1:1 prep should include:

  • Previous notes: Read what you wrote down last time. Did they say they wanted to ship X by this week? Ask about X.
  • One observation: Come with one thing you noticed (positive or constructive). "I saw you step up in that customer call" or "I noticed the last PR took longer than usual—anything stuck?"
  • One question: Ask something personal enough to learn how they're really doing. Not "how are you?" but "What's one thing you want to improve about your own work?"
  • Written summary: Spend 2 minutes after the call writing down outcomes, commitments, and notes. Send it to them.

This sounds formal. But the formality protects the relationship. It says "You're important enough that I'm tracking this."

The Remote Manager's Advantage

Here's the counterintuitive bit: async-first teams can actually have better 1:1s than co-located ones, if they're intentional about it.

When you're not in the same building, you schedule the meeting, clear the calendar, and show up with focus. There are no drive-by interruptions. You can prepare. You can think.

Co-located managers often do 1:1s in hallways, coffee shops, or squeezed between other meetings. They're rushed and reactive.

Remote managers who build a ritual around 1:1s—same time each week, agenda sent 24 hours in advance, notes shared after—often have deeper conversations.

Making It Stick

The easiest way to build this habit is to make it visual and impossible to forget.

Create a template that you use the same way every week. Put it somewhere you see it before each call. Some managers print the structure and post it above their desk. Others use a checklist app. Some put it in a shared doc with their team so everyone knows what to expect.

The point is: your 1:1 structure should be so normal it requires zero willpower to follow.

A visual organizer—one you print and display in your workspace—does this. It reminds you what matters. It signals to your team that you're serious about this meeting. And it gives you something concrete to point to when someone asks "what should we talk about?"

What Comes Next

If you're running a remote team and your 1:1s feel scattered, start with just three things next week:

  1. Write an agenda before each call. Two or three items.
  2. Take notes. Specifically note commitments and follow-ups.
  3. Send a summary (even 3 bullets) after the call.

That alone changes the dynamic. Then, as it becomes routine, you can deepen it—asking bigger questions, tracking growth over quarters, noticing patterns in what people struggle with.

Good 1:1s are the foundation of remote team trust. They're not optional. And they're not hard—they just need structure.
