How to Run Effective One-on-One Meetings

September 15, 2026

One-on-one meetings are where real management happens. Yet most managers either skip them, run them chaotically, or spend 30 minutes listening to status updates they already read in Slack.

The problem is structural. Without a clear format, 1:1s drift. Without clear tracking, action items vanish. Without clear history, you repeat the same conversations month after month.

Remote managers face this even harder: you can't catch someone by their desk, you can't read the room as easily, and async work means the informal check-ins that used to happen naturally just don't. If your 1:1 isn't structured, it becomes another meeting that shouldn't exist.

What a Real 1:1 Should Do

A 1:1 isn't a status update. Your employee should tell you what they're working on before the meeting—in a doc, in a ticket, in a comment. The meeting is for:

  • Clearing blockers they can't remove alone (you)
  • Feedback on work (both ways)
  • Career growth talk (where do they want to go?)
  • Personal context (what's actually going on with them?)

If you're spending 20 minutes explaining what they should already know, the meeting is failing by design.

The Meeting Template That Actually Works

Here's the structure that works for distributed teams:

Before the meeting (24 hours before): - Employee writes down 2-3 things they want to talk about, blockers, or wins from the week - You write down 1-2 pieces of feedback or topics you want to cover - 5 minutes of reading = a meeting that goes somewhere instead of wandering

During the meeting (30-45 minutes):

  1. Check-in (5 min): How are they doing? Not work—them. Remote work erases the human part.
  2. Their priorities (10-15 min): Let them lead. What's on their mind? What do they need from you?
  3. Feedback (5-10 min): Specific, recent, actionable. "Great job on the deck" doesn't stick. "That section on market size changed how I think about our positioning" does.
  4. Blockers and wins (5-10 min): What did they ship? What's stuck? What can you unblock in the next week?
  5. Growth/career (5-10 min): What's next for them? What skill are they trying to build? What opportunity interests them?
  6. Action items (2-3 min): Write down 2-3 things each of you is committing to before the next meeting.

That's the format. It's simple. It scales from a team of 2 to a team of 20.

Why Most 1:1s Fail

No structure: The manager asks "how's it going?" and gets a shrug. 15 minutes of silence. Both people wish they were doing something else.

Status report in disguise: You ask them to go through their tickets. That's what your project tracker is for. You've wasted a synchronous meeting on async information.

No follow-up: You talk about growing them into a tech lead, then never mention it again. They mention a blocker; it doesn't move. No written record, no accountability.

Wrong cadence: Some managers do them monthly or "as needed." Your direct reports need predictability. Weekly is the standard for a reason—it's frequent enough to catch problems and light enough that blocked work doesn't pile up.

No feedback: You only talk to them when something's wrong, or once a quarter in formal reviews. That's not a relationship, that's a performance evaluation. Real feedback happens in context, in real time.

How to Track This So It Actually Sticks

Write everything down. Not in an email thread—in one place.

For each person, keep a rolling record: - Feedback you gave them and what changed - Blockers they mentioned and whether you unblocked them - Career goals and progress toward them - Key decisions made together - Projects they led and the outcome

This serves two purposes: You remember what you talked about (and can reference it), and they see that you're paying attention and following up. Nothing kills trust faster than repeating the same conversation three months later.

If you're managing more than three people, you need a system. A spreadsheet, a notion database, a document per person—something that you actually use and update after each meeting.

The Remote Manager Advantage

Distributed work actually lets you do 1:1s better if you structure them right:

  • Written prep: Both people have to think before the call. No coasting on small talk.
  • Recorded history: Everything is documented. No "I thought we agreed on X."
  • Consistency: You can't skip them because "we're in the same office." Every team member gets the same investment.
  • Async before sync: You clear out the noise before the meeting, so you can focus on what matters.

The only way to mess it up is to treat the meeting like it's optional, or to use it for things that should be emails.

Making It Stick

Start this week. Pick one person on your team. Use the template above. Write down what you talked about and what each of you committed to. Check back in on those items next week.

After three weeks of 1:1s run this way, you'll know whether your people are actually growing, what's blocking them, and where your own focus should be. You'll stop guessing.

Your team is distributed; that's not an excuse for a distributed relationship. Structure wins.
