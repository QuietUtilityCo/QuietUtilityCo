How to Run 1-on-1 Meetings That Actually Drive Results

September 15, 2026

The 1:1 Meeting Problem

Most managers run 1:1s wrong. It's the most common pattern: you block 30 minutes on your calendar, sit down with a report, and either (a) use it as status update time, checking tasks off a list, or (b) let it become a venting session with no forward motion.

The result: your team doesn't feel heard, problems bubble up by surprise, and you lose trust capital week after week.

Remote managers have it harder. You can't read body language as easily. You can't catch someone's frustration in a Slack message. A 1:1 over Zoom is your main lever to understand what's actually going on beneath the surface.

What a Good 1:1 Actually Does

Before structure, understand the purpose. A 1:1 is not: - A status update (that's what your ticket tracker is for) - A feedback session (that's a separate conversation) - A performance review (that's quarterly or annual)

A 1:1 is: - Diagnosis: finding where your report is stuck, blocked, or unclear - Alignment: making sure their work ladder aligns with company goals - Trust: showing you see them as a person with growth potential, not a task executor

When you get those three things right, everything else—retention, motivation, output quality—improves.

The Framework: Open, Dig, Close (30 minutes)

Open (5 minutes)

Start with a genuine question about them as a person, not their work. Not "how's the project going?" but "how are you doing?" or "anything on your mind this week?" Let them set the tone.

Why: You're signaling that you see them as a whole person. Remote work flattens this. Most of your team spends 40 hours staring at their home office wall. A genuine check-in rebuilds the human connection that office proximity used to give you for free.

Listen to their answer. Don't interrupt to pivot to work talk. 90 seconds here prevents months of misunderstanding.

Dig (20 minutes)

Ask one thing: "What's the biggest blocker for you right now—on your current work, your skills, the team, anything?"

Let them answer. If it's vague ("I'm just tired"), ask clarifying questions: "What's draining you?" "Is it a specific project, or is it the pace?" "Do you feel blocked by someone else, or by something about your role?"

The goal is to surface one real problem. It might be: - They don't understand what success looks like - A dependency is slow (waiting on design, blocked by another team) - They're missing a skill and don't want to ask - They're working on something that doesn't matter and nobody told them - They're burned out and considering leaving

Different blockers need different moves from you:

If it's unclear expectations: Go to the whiteboard (or Figma, or Google Doc). Show them the goal. Ask what they think success looks like. Align on metrics.

If it's a dependency: Flag it. Commit to unblocking them. Follow up after the 1:1. Slow dependencies kill momentum and engagement.

If it's a skill gap: Ask if they want to build it. If yes, create a small, bounded project to practice. If no, that's worth knowing—it might mean reassignment or it might mean they're not engaged.

If it's burnout or disengagement: This is the hard one. Don't minimize it. Ask: "What would make this better?" Listen. If the answer is "I need to leave," you'll find out later anyway, but you'll have shown you cared enough to notice.

Close (5 minutes)

Recap what you heard. Commit to one thing you'll do: "I'm going to talk to the design lead and see why you're blocked. Let's touch base Thursday on that."

Ask: "Anything else?" Then end on time. Respecting the 30 minutes teaches your team you value their time.

Why This Breaks Down Remotely (And How to Fix It)

Remote 1:1s often fail because:

The camera creates distance. You're both on mute half the time, checking Slack, half-paying attention. Fix it: turn all cameras on. It's weird at first. Three weeks in, it rebuilds the eye contact and presence you lose.

You can't read the room. Someone's quiet in person, you notice. Over Zoom, they're just a tile. Ask directly: "You're quiet—is that because I'm asking the wrong question, or is there something else?" Directness replaces observation.

You run out of time for this because other stuff crowds it. The fix: block 1:1s in your calendar like they're customer calls. Non-negotiable. Your team's engagement and retention depend on it more than 90% of the meetings you take.

The One Metric That Matters

After your 1:1, ask yourself: "Did I learn something new about what my report actually needs right now, and did I commit to helping?"

If the answer is yes, the 1:1 worked.

If the answer is no, something went wrong. Either you talked too much, or you didn't dig deep enough, or you treated it like admin time.

Run the next one differently.

What Happens When You Get It Right

After 3-4 weeks of 1:1s run this way:

  • Your team starts asking you harder questions in the meetings (signal: they trust you)
  • Surprises drop (you see problems coming)
  • Your best people stay longer (they feel seen)
  • Your worst people leave faster (and that's good—misalignment happens earlier)
  • Problems get solved faster because people tell you about them instead of working around them

That's worth 30 minutes a week.

If you're managing a remote team and you're losing good people or your team feels disconnected, the 1:1 is the lever. Fix it first, before you hire another manager or restructure the org.

Most manager problems are actually 1:1 problems in disguise.
