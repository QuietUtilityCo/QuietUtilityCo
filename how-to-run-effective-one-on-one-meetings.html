How to Run Effective One-on-One Meetings

September 15, 2026

Why 1:1 Meetings Matter

One-on-one meetings are where real management happens. They're your private channel to understand what's working, what's blocking your team, and what your people actually need to succeed. Yet most managers run them badly—no structure, no follow-up, and by next week they've forgotten half of what was discussed.

The cost of poor 1:1s is real. Action items slip through the cracks. Concerns get buried. People feel unheard. Turnover increases. And for remote and distributed teams, 1:1s are often the only meaningful synchronous contact you have with each direct report.

Structure Your 1:1s

A good 1:1 should have three clear sections:

1. Check-in (5 minutes) Start by asking how they're doing. Not just "how's work?" but genuine interest. Are they stressed? Energized? Struggling at home? A person's state matters. You can't address performance issues if you don't understand context.

2. Updates and Blockers (10-15 minutes) Let them drive the agenda here. What are they working on? What's working? What's stuck? This is where you catch problems early—resource gaps, unclear priorities, or interpersonal friction on the team. Listen more than you talk.

3. Goals and Growth (5-10 minutes) Circle back to their career development. What are they learning? What's next for them? Are they on track with their goals this quarter? This is also where you can flag your own feedback or concerns.

Document Everything

Here's the gap most managers have: they don't document 1:1s systematically. You run a great conversation on Tuesday, but by Thursday you've forgotten the action item. Your direct report thinks you don't care because you never followed up.

Keep a simple running record for each person: - Date and key topics discussed - What they said matters to them (blockers, goals, concerns) - Action items with owners and deadlines - Commitments you made (resources, introductions, feedback) - Notes on their development (skills they're building, gaps to address)

This serves two purposes. First, it forces you to actually follow through. Second, it becomes a real record when you need it—for performance conversations, promotion decisions, or severance documentation.

Track Action Items Across Your Team

If you manage 5+ people, you need a way to track what you've committed to for each person. Otherwise you'll over-commit to your star performer and under-deliver to the person who needs support most.

A simple spreadsheet works: one row per person, columns for: - Open action items (with dates) - Feedback you owe them - Development goals they're tracking - Last 1:1 date - Next scheduled 1:1

Review it before each 1:1. It takes 30 seconds and prevents you from running the same conversation twice or forgetting a critical follow-up.

Make Them Consistent

Schedule 1:1s the same day and time each week if possible. Consistency builds psychological safety. People know when they can expect your full attention. Remote teams especially need this predictability.

Treat the time as non-negotiable. Don't reschedule for meetings. If you do, you're signaling that connecting with your people is optional.

The Real Win

The managers who see the biggest improvements in team engagement and retention are the ones who treat 1:1s like a system, not a conversation. Structure + documentation + follow-through = people who feel heard and managed well.

You don't need fancy software. A spreadsheet and a calendar block will change your team dynamic in 30 days.
