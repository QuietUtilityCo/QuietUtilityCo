How to Run Effective One-on-One Meetings

September 15, 2026

One-on-one meetings are your best tool for keeping a remote team together. Done right, they catch problems before they spiral, build real trust, and give people a space to think out loud. Done wrong, they're 30 minutes of awkward silence and bland updates.

The difference is usually structure.

Why Structure Matters for 1:1s

When you run 1:1s without a clear format, three things happen: First, they drift into status updates ("What did you do this week?") that waste everyone's time and could be a Slack message. Second, you miss the actual problems—the person who's frustrated with a process, the one stuck on a blocker, the one looking for feedback on an idea. Third, you end each meeting feeling like you covered nothing important but somehow spent the whole 30 minutes.

Remote work makes this worse. In an office, you notice when someone's quiet or distracted. Over Zoom, you miss it. A structured 1:1 framework forces both of you to be intentional about what matters.

The Three-Part Structure

Part 1: Personal Check-in (5 minutes)

Start with something that isn't work. How's your week going? Anything on your mind outside of work? This serves two purposes: It gives the person a moment to mention something blocking them mentally (sick kid, home renovation disaster, just tired) without awkwardness. And it builds actual human connection, which remote teams desperately need.

Don't skip this. People perform better when they feel seen.

Part 2: Their Priorities & Blockers (15 minutes)

This is where you listen. Ask: - What are you focused on this week? - Anything blocking you or slowing you down? - Do you have what you need to move forward? - Is there anything you need from me?

Write down blockers. If someone mentions the same blocker two weeks in a row and you haven't helped clear it, you've found a management failure.

Part 3: Your Input & Development (10 minutes)

This is your chance to give feedback, raise something you've noticed, or discuss their growth. But frame it as a conversation, not a lecture. If you noticed they handled a client situation well, say so. If you saw a pattern in how they handle feedback, talk about it.

Use this time to discuss: - Feedback on recent work or behavior - Career development or skills to build - Upcoming opportunities or projects they might own

What Makes Remote 1:1s Actually Work

Keep them weekly. Bi-weekly sounds reasonable but creates two problems: You forget what happened last time, and small problems compound into bigger ones. Weekly is 30 minutes. It's not a lot.

Same day and time every week. Consistency removes negotiation friction and signals that you prioritize this. Your direct report shouldn't have to ask you when the next 1:1 is.

Use a shared document. Before the meeting, your direct report can drop in notes: wins from the week, things they want to talk about, blockers. You can add notes too. Between meetings, you both can reference what you discussed and track progress on blockers. This becomes your accountability tool.

Stay off email during the meeting. If you're typing or skimming other stuff, the person across from you knows. It destroys trust. Close the tabs. Treat 30 minutes as sacred.

Don't cancel. Cancelling a 1:1 sends a message: This isn't important. That person will stop bringing real problems to you. Don't do it. If you must reschedule, reschedule within 2-3 days.

The Blocker List is Your Management Tool

After three months of weekly 1:1s with structure, you'll have a list of blockers that show up repeatedly: - Same person always blocked waiting for design feedback - Two teams can't talk to each other - Someone's unclear on their responsibilities - A tool everyone hates is slowing work down

This list is gold. It tells you exactly what to fix as a manager. Most managers run 1:1s for months and never extract this signal because they're not tracking it anywhere.

Keep a running document. Every blocker mentioned goes in. Mark when it's resolved. When you see a blocker hit twice, it becomes a priority. When you see the same category of blocker across three people, it's a systemic problem you need to solve.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Turning it into a status meeting. If you want status, ask for a written update. Use the 1:1 to dig deeper and talk about what matters.

Giving all feedback in the 1:1. Quick feedback should happen in the moment. The 1:1 is for bigger patterns and bigger conversations, not "You forgot to CC the client on that email."

Not taking notes. You will forget what you discussed. Write it down. Your direct report will notice you remember what they said, and it matters.

Talking the whole time. Your job is to listen. If you're talking more than 40% of the time, you're doing it wrong.

Skipping them when things are "going well." This is backwards. When things are going well, the 1:1 is where you reinforce what's working. When things are hard, it's the place to troubleshoot together.

Setting Up Your System

Start with a simple shared document or spreadsheet. Each week, before the meeting: - Person writes: This week's focus, blockers, wins, what they need - You write: Feedback, topics you want to cover

During the meeting, take notes in a shared visible place. After, you have a record of what you discussed and what was agreed.

After 4-6 weeks of consistent 1:1s with this structure, you'll notice the difference: People bring you problems earlier. You catch issues before they become expensive. Your team feels more aligned because you're actually talking about what matters instead of surface-level updates.

That's how remote teams actually stay together.
