1:1 Meeting Checklist for Managers: Template & Best Practices

September 15, 2026

Running effective 1:1 meetings is the single highest-leverage activity a manager can do—yet most managers wing it. Without structure, 1:1s drift into status updates or feel awkward, and opportunities to develop your people, uncover problems, and build trust get missed.

A good 1:1 checklist keeps you on track, ensures consistency across your team, and makes every meeting count. Here's what belongs in yours.

What a 1:1 Meeting Checklist Should Cover

A solid checklist has five parts:

Before the meeting: Send an agenda 24 hours ahead so your direct report can prepare. Block the time on both calendars and make it recurring—consistency matters more than frequency. Schedule in a quiet place where you won't be interrupted.

Opening (first 2 minutes): Start with small talk. Not because it's mandatory, but because context switching is real. Your person just came from a Slack thread or another meeting. Give them 60 seconds to settle. Then ask "What's on your mind?" or "How are you feeling this week?" Don't jump straight into your agenda.

Wins and blockers (5 minutes): Have your direct report share one recent win (something they shipped, improved, or solved) and one blocker (something slowing them down). This is diagnostic: it tells you what's working, what energy they're spending, and where they need help. Write these down—you'll reference them in future meetings.

Career and development (5 minutes): Ask one of these each week, rotating: - "What skill do you want to build in the next 3 months?" - "How do you feel about your growth here?" - "Is there a project or area you'd like more exposure to?" - "What's one thing I could do differently as your manager?"

One question per meeting. This prevents death by questionnaire and builds psychological safety when you genuinely act on feedback.

Feedback and coaching (3 minutes): If you have specific feedback (positive or corrective), deliver it now while context is fresh. Use the situation-behavior-impact format: "In the meeting yesterday, when you disagreed with the design without asking questions first, it made the designer feel unheard and slowed the discussion." Then listen. If there's nothing specific, skip this—forced feedback is noise.

Closing and next steps (2 minutes): Recap what you discussed, clarify any actions or decisions, and confirm the next meeting time. You should walk out of every 1:1 with one thing your report will do and one thing you will do.

Common Mistakes That Kill 1:1s

Skipping them under deadline pressure. Your instinct is wrong. 1:1s reduce deadline risk by surfacing blockers and keeping people aligned. Cancel a meeting; don't skip your 1:1.

Letting your direct report control the entire agenda. Status updates are efficient; they're not high-leverage. You need at least one question about development, blockers, or how they're feeling. If the meeting is all updates, you're managing outputs, not people.

Asking "Any issues?" when you haven't built trust yet. Early 1:1s, people won't volunteer problems. Ask specific questions about recent work: "How did that integration project feel?" or "What was the hardest part of that feature?"

Sharing feedback without context. "You interrupted too much in standup" lands badly if your person doesn't remember the specific moment. Use the situation-behavior-impact format and tie it to one incident.

Not documenting anything. You'll forget. After six months, you won't remember patterns in what they struggle with, what excites them, or what you promised to fix. Write one or two lines after every 1:1.

Why Your 1:1 Checklist Matters for Revenue

If you run a team, your effectiveness is their effectiveness. People who feel heard, get clear feedback, and see a path forward stay longer, take on bigger work, and need less supervision. That reduces turnover cost, improves shipping speed, and compounds over quarters.

A one-page checklist isn't a perfect substitute for judgment—you still need to listen and adapt each meeting to the person. But it's the difference between a 1:1 that matters and a 1:1 that wastes both of your time.
