How to Run 1:1 Meetings That Actually Drive Results

September 15, 2026

Running a 1:1 with no structure is like driving with no destination. You show up, talk about work, maybe complain about the sprint, then both walk away unsure what changed.

Remote managers often inherit this problem—no in-person culture to fall back on, no hallway conversations to catch small issues before they become big ones. A poorly run 1:1 wastes 30 minutes and leaves both manager and report frustrated.

Why Your 1:1s Probably Aren't Working

Most managers make one of three mistakes:

1. No agenda. You call the meeting with vague intent: "Let's sync." The report has no chance to prepare. You spend 20 minutes on status updates that should have been Slack messages, leaving 10 minutes for anything that actually matters.

2. You do all the talking. You download your priorities, give feedback, ask a few questions, then sign off. The report learned nothing about growth, and you missed what's actually blocking them.

3. No follow-up. You agree to help them with a blocker. Next meeting, you don't mention it again. They notice you forgot, trust erodes, and they stop bringing real problems to the table.

These habits compound. Over six months, your team stops preparing for 1:1s. They give surface-level answers. You're managing by guessing, not by data. Turnover happens quietly.

A Structure That Works

High-performing teams use a simple framework: Check-In → Priorities → Growth → Admin.

1. Check-In (5 minutes)

Start with a real question. Not "How are you?" but something specific: - "What's one thing that went well this week?" - "What did you learn this week that surprised you?" - "What's draining your energy right now?"

This takes 5 minutes but signals: I care about you as a person, not just your output. Remote teams especially need this. It replaces the human contact that in-office teams get by accident.

What to do: Write the question in the meeting invite 24 hours before. It gives them time to think of a real answer instead of a corporate platitude.

2. Priorities (10 minutes)

Your report talks first. Ask: "What are your three main priorities for the next two weeks?"

Listen without interrupting. If they list more than three, ask them to cut. This forces clarity—for them and for you. You'll often learn that your priorities and theirs don't align. That's the point.

What to do: Write down their three priorities in a shared doc during the call. At the end of the meeting, read them back. "Does this capture it?" This simple act drops misalignment down by 40% in most teams.

3. Growth (10 minutes)

Ask: "What's a skill or area you want to improve in the next quarter?"

This is not feedback. It's their agenda. They might say: "I want to get better at writing specs" or "I need to learn SQL."

Your job is to: - Remove blockers. If they want to learn SQL, do they have time to take a course? Can they pair with the data team? - Give them a bet. "Run a 3-week experiment. Work with the data team one hour a week. Then we'll talk about what stuck."

Bets are more memorable than advice. They own the outcome.

What to do: At each 1:1, ask how the last growth bet is going. "How's that SQL learning going?" This shows you actually remembered and care.

4. Admin (5 minutes)

Anything that doesn't fit above. A policy change, a project shift, feedback from stakeholders.

Keep it brief. This is not feedback time. If you have real feedback on their work, it should have happened during Priorities, or in a separate conversation—never at the end of the meeting when they're mentally checked out.

The Numbers That Matter

After implementing this structure, teams typically see:

  • 30% fewer status meeting requests (because the 1:1 actually covers priorities)
  • Faster blocker resolution (because you're hearing them earlier)
  • Lower turnover (because people feel heard)
  • Better alignment (because you're literally writing down their priorities together)

One manager went from "What did you ship?" conversations to learning that her backend engineer was blocked waiting for a design review for two weeks. Nobody had asked. Removing that blocker saved the project timeline.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Don't multitask. If you're checking email during their 1:1, they will notice and disengage. Camera on, phone away.

Don't give feedback in the moment. If they tell you about a mistake, don't spiral into a teaching moment. Write it down. Talk about it in context later, not in a 1:1 meant for connection and growth.

Don't skip the growth section. This is where retention happens. People leave because they're stuck, not because they hate the job.

Don't make it transactional. If every 1:1 is you assigning work, they'll dread it. Balance: 50% about them, 50% about the work.

Your Next 1:1

Try this structure on your next report: 1. Open with the check-in question (send it 24 hours before). 2. Let them own priorities for 10 minutes. Write them down. 3. Ask about growth. Make a specific bet. 4. Handle admin. Done in 30 minutes.

After three cycles, your reports will come more prepared. They'll answer deeper questions. You'll catch blockers faster. The meeting goes from a formality to a place where real work gets unblocked.

The difference between a manager who gets results and one who doesn't isn't usually talent—it's structure.
