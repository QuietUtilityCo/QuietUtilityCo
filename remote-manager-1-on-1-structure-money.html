How to Run 1:1s That Actually Make You Money

September 15, 2026

Most remote managers run 1:1s on autopilot. They block 30 minutes on the calendar, open a video call, ask "How's it going?", listen to whatever gets volunteered, maybe drop a piece of feedback, then move on. Eight weeks later they're shocked their best people leave without warning.

The cost is brutal. Replacing a single mid-level employee runs $15k–$40k depending on role. A team of eight with 25% annual attrition is losing $30k–$100k per year to avoidable turnover. That's real money leaving the building because you didn't structure a conversation properly.

The Problem: 1:1s Feel Like a Waste Because They Are Unstructured

Your directs don't show up with agenda items. You don't have a framework. There's no continuity from week to week. The conversation drifts into complaints about infrastructure, then Slack burns, then they say "everything's fine" and the meeting ends. You get no signal about whether they're actually engaged, burned out, planning to leave, or ready for more responsibility.

You also miss the biggest lever: discovery about what actually makes them stay.

Ninety percent of managers ask about performance. Smart ones ask about career, learning, compensation, autonomy, fit with team values—and then they actually act on what they hear. People stay for managers who listen and move. People leave for managers who nod and do nothing.

The Architecture of a Real 1:1: The Four Sections

Steal this structure. Do it every week. It takes 30 minutes if you're disciplined.

1. Wins + Energy (5 min)

Start here. Ask: "What's one thing you shipped or helped land this week? How's your energy?" Not fake positivity—genuine signal.

Why? People need to be heard on wins before they'll be honest about friction. Also, energy tells you everything. If someone with a great week sounds flat, something's wrong (teammate conflict, comp resentment, interview they just did). If they sound energized even on a hard week, they're engaged.

2. Blockers + Asks (10 min)

"What's in your way? What do you need from me, the team, or the company?"

Let them drive this. Your job is to write it down and close it or explain why you can't. This is where trust gets built or burned. A manager who says "I'll look into that" and forgets breaks the person. A manager who says "That's blocked by X, here's my plan to unblock it" or "That's not a priority for us, here's why" builds credibility.

3. Growth + Future (10 min)

"What skill do you want to develop? Where do you see yourself in 12 months? What would make this job feel more like your future?"

This is the retention lever. People don't leave jobs—they leave managers who can't map a path. If your direct says "I want to learn systems design" and you say "Cool, let's pair you with X on the next architecture review," you've just made the job more valuable than the outside offer they were considering.

Also: use this to spot when someone's planning to jump. Someone who suddenly says "I don't know" after months of clear ambition has often started interviewing. That's your signal to either move mountains on compensation, scope, or growth, or accept you're going to lose them—so you can start backfill planning.

4. Relationship (5 min)

"Anything non-work you want to share? How's your life?"

This is the humanity check. You're not a therapist, but you're a human who works with them. Knowing someone's kid started school, their partner just got a promotion, or they've got a health thing going on makes you a manager, not a scheduler.

The Weekly Record: Your Retention Early Warning System

After each 1:1, spend two minutes documenting: - One thing they're excited about - One blocker they mentioned - One growth area or future goal
- Their energy level (1–5) - Any asks you committed to—and the date you'll close them

Do this in a shared doc or simple spreadsheet. When you see the same blocker twice, that's a pattern—fix it or explain it.

When someone's energy dips below 3 for two consecutive weeks, schedule a 30-min conversation with them: "I'm noticing you seem flat. What's actually going on?" You're either going to fix something real or find out they're interviewing—and you can act.

When someone who wants to learn X never gets a project on X, that's a live attrition risk. Move it to your quarterly planning. Give them the shot.

Why This Costs You Real Money If You Don't Do It

One checkout: $25k+ in recruiting and lost productivity.
Two checkouts: $50k+ and your team's morale drops (people see others leaving without explanation).
Three in a year from a team of eight: You're at $75k spent, plus you're scrambling to backfill, plus your remaining people feel unsupported.

A remote manager running structured 1:1s sees: - 30–50% lower unplanned attrition - Earlier signal on high performers ready for scope/comp increases (so you keep them) - Team that trusts you because you actually move on their asks - Better hiring instincts (you know what people actually value, so you screen for it)

The Implementation: Start This Week

Pick one person. Run the 1:1 with this structure this week. Write down the four sections. Notice what you learn that you wouldn't have learned with "how's it going?"

Then do it again next week with everyone. By week four it's automatic. By month two you'll have four weeks of data on every direct—what they care about, where they're at risk, where they're ready to grow.

That's how you turn a 30-minute status meeting into a $30k retention tool.

The people who leave companies without this system are often the ones you wish you'd kept. Don't be that manager.
