Run 1:1 Meetings That Actually Matter

September 15, 2026

One-on-ones are the most underutilized tool in a remote manager's toolkit. They're scheduled, they happen—and they're often a waste of 30 minutes.

The problem isn't that you're not doing them. It's that most 1:1s follow the same broken pattern: status updates, quick logistics, done. Your direct report leaves without feeling heard. You miss the actual problems brewing beneath the surface. The relationship stays transactional.

Worse: in a remote environment, the 1:1 is often your only real touchpoint with someone. It's doing the work of hallway conversations, casual check-ins, and relationship-building all at once. When it fails, everything suffers.

Why Standard 1:1s Fail

Most managers run 1:1s the way they inherited them—or worse, they skip them entirely when things get busy.

The default pattern looks like this: - First 5 minutes: small talk or logistical catch-up - Next 15 minutes: the person updates you on their projects - Last 10 minutes: you address an urgent work issue or company news - End: vague "let me know if you need anything" and close the tab

What you're not doing: understanding what's actually blocking them, learning what they want from their role, noticing patterns in their confidence or engagement, building a relationship strong enough that they feel safe bringing you hard problems.

The cost compounds. People leave because their manager didn't know them. Mistakes happen because warning signs were missed. High performers plateau because nobody invested in their growth. Your influence as a leader never deepens.

The Framework That Works

Better 1:1s follow a simple structure, but one that requires intentionality.

Before the meeting: Have a short note ready—not a checklist, but 2-3 observations about what you've seen that week. "I noticed your energy was different in Tuesday's meeting" or "That code review you did for Sarah was really thorough." This isn't flattery; it's specificity. It signals that you're actually paying attention.

First 5 minutes—Open the door: Don't jump into work. Ask a real question: "How are you actually doing?" and listen. Not for status updates. For tone. For what they volunteer. Remote work hides a lot. Someone can seem fine in Slack and be burned out. This is where you notice.

Next 15 minutes—Their agenda: Ask: "What's on your mind this week?" Let them lead. This is their 1:1, not yours. If they say "nothing," ask more specifically: "What's one thing that felt hard?" or "What are you thinking about for your own growth?"

Next 7 minutes—Your observations: Share what you noticed. "I saw you shipped that feature faster than usual—tell me what changed." Or "The client feedback mentioned X, and I thought of you because I remember you were concerned about that." Again: specificity and attention.

Last 3 minutes—Forward look: One thing: "What's one thing you want to focus on before we talk again?" or "What would make next week feel successful for you?" Write it down. Reference it next time.

This isn't a to-do list meeting. It's a relationship meeting. The work happens because the relationship is strong.

Common Mistakes That Kill 1:1s

Running late or skipping: The signal you send is "this isn't a priority." People internalize that. Remote managers have to protect the 1:1 the way office managers naturally protect it. Calendar blocking is non-negotiable.

Making it about you: Spending 25 minutes talking about the company's latest pivot, your stress level, or what you need from them. Their 1:1 is about them. If you need to align on something, do it, then flip back.

Asking questions you already know the answer to: "How's the project coming?" when you've seen the Jira board. People sense when they're being tested rather than connected with. Ask what you actually don't know.

No follow-up: You say "I'll look into that" and never mention it again. The message: your concerns don't matter enough to track. Keep a running note of what they brought up and what happened next.

Staying surface-level: Asking "how are you?" and moving on when they say "fine." Ask again. "What's one thing that felt hard this week?" or "What would make you feel more supported?" Depth requires gentle persistence.

Why This Matters for Remote Teams

In an office, you get passive information: overhearing conversations, reading someone's body language in meetings, casual hallway check-ins. Remote work strips that away. The 1:1 becomes the source of real information about what's actually happening with someone.

Managers who master it notice problems early, develop stronger loyalty, catch high performers before competitors do, and make better coaching decisions. Managers who don't end up surprised when good people leave, miss warning signs, and never build the influence that compounds over years.

The good news: this doesn't require more time. It requires different time. The same 30 minutes, structured differently, changes everything.

Next Steps

If your 1:1s feel stale, start here: 1. Before your next 1:1, write down two specific observations about that person's work or growth. 2. Ask one question you genuinely don't know the answer to. 3. Let them talk. Don't fill silence. 4. Write down what they said mattered to them. Reference it next time.

The pattern compounds. After three or four cycles of this, the relationship shifts. They trust you more. You know them better. The conversations get deeper. And the work gets better.
