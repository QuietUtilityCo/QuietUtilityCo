How to Calculate True DTC Profitability (Not Just Gross Margin)

September 15, 2026

Most DTC brands are lying to themselves about profitability. You look at a $100 sale, see $60 in gross profit, and think the business is healthy. Meanwhile, you're spending $80 to acquire that customer, and you'll burn through 18 months of runway before they repeat-buy.

This is the difference between gross margin (how much each sale makes before marketing) and true profitability (whether the whole business makes money). Most DTC founders optimize for the first and ignore the second.

By the time you realize the unit economics don't work, you've already hired a team, leased a warehouse, and spent six months building a business that can't scale profitably.

The Five Metrics That Actually Matter

1. Contribution Margin (Not Gross Margin)

Gross margin is revenue minus COGS. It tells you how much money each product sale generates before you spend anything on customer acquisition.

Contribution margin is gross profit minus variable customer acquisition cost. It tells you what's left over for operations and profit.

Example: - Product sells for $100 - COGS: $25 - Gross profit: $75 (75% gross margin—looks great!) - Ad spend to acquire: $80 - Contribution margin: -$5 per customer

You're actually losing money on every sale. Your gross margin was lying to you.

If you're not tracking contribution margin by channel, you don't know which parts of your business are actually profitable. Facebook might have a 60% gross margin but a -$10 contribution margin because you're overspending on ads.

Benchmark: Healthy DTC should have positive contribution margin on every major channel. If it's negative, pause that channel until you improve either pricing, product cost, or ad efficiency.

2. EBITDA Margin (Your Real Profit Line)

EBITDA = Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, Amortization.

In plain English: (Gross Profit) - (Operating Expenses).

This is what's left after you pay for: - Salaries - Software subscriptions - Fulfillment and shipping - Warehousing - Customer service - Accounting and legal

Many DTC brands run at 60-70% gross margin but 0-5% EBITDA margin. Why? Because they hired a 5-person team to manage $1M in revenue.

At $1M revenue with a 65% gross margin, you have $650k in gross profit. If your team + operations cost $600k/year, your EBITDA margin is only 8%. Growth looks healthy; profit is razor-thin.

Benchmark: Healthy DTC at $3-10M revenue should target 10-25% EBITDA margin. Below 5% and you're not sustainable. At 2-3% you're one bad month away from layoffs.

3. Cost Per Acquisition (CPA) vs. Customer Lifetime Value (LTV)

CPA is how much you spend in ads to acquire one customer.

LTV is how much profit you'll make from that customer over their entire relationship with you.

The ratio between them is everything.

If your CPA is $50 and your LTV is $200, you have a healthy 4:1 ratio. For every dollar you spend acquiring a customer, you make $4 in lifetime value.

But most DTC brands don't know their LTV. They estimate it, or worse, they confuse first-order value with lifetime value.

Real LTV calculation: - Average order value: $100 - Repeat purchase rate: 30% - Average customer lifespan: 2 years - Gross margin per order: $65

LTV = $100 × (1 + repeat rate) × lifespan × margin = $100 × 1.3 × 2 × 0.65 = $169

If you're spending $80 to acquire that customer (CPA = $80), your LTV:CPA ratio is 2.1:1. That's cutting it close. Below 2.5:1, you're at risk. Above 5:1, you're likely underspending on growth.

Benchmark: Aim for 3:1 to 5:1 LTV:CAC ratio.

4. CAC Payback Period

This is how long it takes for a customer's purchases to pay back the cost of acquiring them.

If your CPA is $80 and your customer's average monthly repeat order value (net of COGS) is $20, your payback period is 4 months.

Why does this matter?

Cash flow. If you're spending $100k/month on ads and your CAC payback is 6 months, you need a $300k cash buffer just to not run out of money. At a 3-month payback, you only need $150k.

DTC founders with short payback periods can reinvest profit immediately. DTC founders with long payback periods need either venture capital or they'll exhaust their runway.

Benchmark: Aim for 3-6 month payback. Over 8 months and you're either underpriceed or have poor repeat rates.

5. Repeat Purchase Rate (The Lever Nobody Pulls)

The single highest-leverage metric most DTC founders ignore.

A business with a 10% repeat rate will die. A business with a 40% repeat rate will print money.

Why? Because repeat customers are 5-10x cheaper to acquire (you use email, SMS, and social retargeting, not paid ads). Their lifetime value is 3x higher. Their margin is the same.

If you improve repeat purchase rate from 20% to 30%, your LTV goes up 50%. Your CAC payback period drops 33%. You can cut ad spend and grow faster.

Most DTC brands can improve repeat rate by: - Better onboarding emails (40% of customers don't realize they should repeat-buy) - SMS reminders when they run out (subscription or perpetual reminder) - Loyalty program (5-10% incremental repeat) - Product quality (fixes the real blocker)

Benchmark: E-commerce usually targets 20-40%. Consumables should hit 40-60%. If you're below 15%, your brand has a product or messaging problem, not a demand problem.

How to Build the System

You need a spreadsheet (or tool) that captures: 1. Every transaction: date, revenue, channel, COGS, customer 2. Every ad dollar spent, mapped to channel 3. Customer emails so you can track repeats 4. Monthly roll-ups of the metrics above

Most founders use fragmented sheets: one for ad spend (Facebook), one for revenue (Shopify), one for COGS (supplier invoice), and they try to map them manually. By the time the numbers are in the system, they're a month old and the decision is already made.

Build the system once, feed it weekly data, and watch the business transform. Within 60 days of accurate LTV:CAC tracking, you'll spot your most profitable channel. Within 90 days, you'll know exactly where to cut or scale.

The Hard Part: Discipline

The hard part isn't the math. It's that these metrics force you to see the truth.

You'll realize that your "successful" $2M revenue year is actually losing money when you factor in the salaries you paid yourself and your team.

You'll see that your best-performing ad channel is actually your worst because you're paying for first-time buyers, not repeats.

You'll discover that your product's repeat rate is 8% when it should be 25%, and no amount of customer acquisition will fix that.

But once you see it, you can act. Cut costs. Improve product. Raise prices. Pause channels. Hire. Scale the right levers.

The brands that make it aren't the ones with the biggest gross margins. They're the ones that face their unit economics squarely and fix them before the runway ends.
